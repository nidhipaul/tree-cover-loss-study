Kenya's forest carbon ledger closes 5.7M tonnes CO₂e in the black every year

At >30% canopy cover, Kenya's 300 sub-counties remove an estimated 14.0 million tonnes of CO₂e a year and emit 8.3 million — a net sink of 5.7 million tonnes. That national balance nets out two very different ledgers: Mau Forest sub-counties in Narok and Nakuru running deep in the red, offset by the coastal forests of Lamu and Ijara and the Mount Kenya slopes of Nyeri banking the largest surpluses. Count dense forest alone, at >75%, and the national balance tips into a small net source.

What does Canopy cover >30% mean?

How thick did the tree cover have to be in 2000 for a patch of land to count as forest at all? Every 30 m square of Kenya has a score for how much of it sat under canopy that year — 5%, 40%, 90%. The threshold is where you draw the line. At >30%, only squares that were at least 30% covered are counted; the rest are ignored.

It defines forest. It says nothing about how much was later lost. Set the line low and patchy scrub counts too, so the totals grow; set it high and only dense forest counts.

>30% is the standard that gets published.

Kenya's Constitution targets 10% tree cover of total land area — that is a share of the country, not a canopy density, so it is not this setting.

How to read a carbon ledger

Carbon stock is the aboveground biomass carbon standing in a sub-county's forest in 2000, in megagrams of carbon (Mg C).

Gross emissions are CO₂-equivalent released when that biomass is cleared, burned or decays, averaged per year.

Gross removals are CO₂e drawn down by forest growth over the same period.

Net flux is removals minus emissions: negative is a net sink (more carbon absorbed than released), positive is a net source.

A sub-county can show heavy tree cover loss and still be a net sink, if regrowth elsewhere in its forest outpaces what was cleared — and the reverse also holds.

Carbon flux — Harris, N.L., D.A. Gibbs, A. Baccini, et al. 2021, Nature Climate Change 11: 234–240; revised in Gibbs, D.A., M. Rose, et al. 2025, ESSD 17(3): 1217–1243.
